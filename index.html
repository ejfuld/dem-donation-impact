2026 Impact Ranker

Which competitive U.S. House and Senate races give a Democratic donor the most impact per dollar — right now.

Impact here is how much the next dollar donated is expected to move the chance of flipping that seat and its chamber, compared to donating that dollar to a randomly chosen Democratic nominee for Congress in 2026 (i.e. the average). So 10x means your dollar goes 10x as far as a donation to the average Democratic candidate this year.

Like all models, this one is based on assumptions. If you disagree with my assumptions, adjust the sliders below to see how the results change under different ones.

 

Only U.S. citizens and green-card holders may legally donate to U.S. federal political campaigns. If that's not you but you'd still like to help, many campaigns also take volunteers — check the candidate's own website.

How much extra, measured in House seats, the tipping-point seat that decides control of the House is worth. At 100, winning the seat that flips the chamber is worth 100 ordinary House seats on top of the seat itself.

Set high because the House is purely majoritarian and every seat is contested every cycle: flipping the seat that flips the House is the whole game. There is no equivalent of a filibuster threshold or a staggered six-year term to blunt it — the majority either exists or it does not.

How much the tipping-point Senate seat is worth relative to the House control weight. 1.0 means the same control bonus applies to both chambers.

Default 0.5 because a non-tipping Senate seat can still move the tipping point in the next two cycles, and because the Senate has thresholds beyond a bare majority (60 for cloture, two-thirds for treaties and impeachment convictions), whereas the House is purely majoritarian with every seat contested every cycle — so bare control of the House is the sharper prize, and the Senate's control bonus is set at half of it.

What this is

Which FEC-derived figure stands in for "how funded is this campaign already" when computing diminishing returns, before the outside-money multiplier above is added on top. Cash on hand + projected contributions extrapolates each campaign's recent monthly fundraising rate out to Election Day; total receipts is everything raised so far this cycle; cash on hand is only what's currently banked. Whichever figure is chosen is used as reported, with no minimum floor — it's a fixed model constant, not adjustable here, that keeps a campaign with little or no reported money from being scored as having infinitely valuable first dollars (see Methodology below).

Methodology

The model. For each race, let a be the modeled probability that one additional vote flips that seat, and b be the modeled probability that one additional vote in that seat flips control of its chamber. A race's total value is V = c_eff·b + w·a, where w is 1 for a House seat or the Senate-seat-value slider for a Senate seat, and c_eff is the House control weight for a House seat, or the House control weight times the Senate control multiplier for a Senate seat (c_eff = c_house·senate_mult).

a — chance one vote flips the seat. Silver Bulletin's forecast gives a win probability for the leading Democrat (or, where there is none, the leading independent). That probability is converted to an implied margin distribution (assumed roughly normal, with a race-specific spread derived from Silver's own elasticity estimate), and a is the density of that margin distribution at a perfectly tied race, times one over the size of the race's electorate. Intuitively: a race Silver already calls near a toss-up, with relatively few voters, has a much higher chance that a single vote is decisive than a lopsided race or one with a huge electorate.

b — chance one vote flips the chamber. b = a × tipping, where tipping is Silver Bulletin's own modeled probability that this specific seat ends up being the tipping-point seat for chamber control. That is, b is built here as P(one vote flips this seat) × P(this seat is the tipping point), assuming those two events are independent. They aren't, in reality: a race close enough to be flipped by one vote is, on average, also more likely than a random seat to be the one deciding the chamber, so this decomposition understates the true chamber term. c_house (and, for a Senate seat, senate_mult) is the compensating dial — raise it to put more weight on the chamber-control term than this understated b alone would justify.

Reach cost, and turning it into dollars. A dollar spent on broadcast advertising pays for impressions across an entire media market, not just the district, so in a district that shares an expensive market with other, more populous or more numerous constituencies, much of that spending reaches voters who cannot cast a vote in this race. Reach cost (reach) is that per-constituent media-market overspill factor multiplied by the relative size of the race's own electorate (median House district = 1.0) — in other words, what it actually costs a campaign to put a message in front of a fixed share, here 1%, of its electorate. The theta slider blends that race-specific reach cost with anchor, the mean reach cost across scorable House races — a stand-in for "fully targetable, priced like an average race" spending: P_dollars = (theta·anchor + (1−theta)·reach) × K. K ($5,000) converts that index into an actual dollar figure — the price of one full "impression pass," touching every voter in the race once — calibrated at roughly $0.02 per impression times a ~250,000-voter median House district. So P_dollars is a real cost in dollars, not just an index: at the defaults, it runs from under $20,000 in the cheapest races to well over $100,000 in the most expensive ones.

Electorate size (Nrel). A dollar doesn't buy a fixed head count of persuaded voters, it buys a fixed fraction of a pass — the same theta/reach-priced share of the race's electorate regardless of that electorate's size. But that fraction of a bigger electorate reaches more actual people, so raw is scaled up by Nrel, the race's electorate size relative to the median House district. Net effect: electorate size cancels out of the seat term rather than mechanically favoring small districts, which is what would happen if a dollar instead bought a fixed number of persuaded voters.

Outside money, weighted down. Party committees and super PACs also spend on a candidate's behalf, and that spending already reaches voters — but a dollar of it doesn't buy as much reach as a dollar the candidate's own committee spends, because federal law gives candidates (not outside groups) the lowest unit charge for broadcast time in the 60 days before an election. money_eff = M + outside_mult × outside_total, where M is whichever of the candidate's own money figures is selected below and outside_total is party-committee plus super-PAC spending supporting or opposing the candidate. The "Outside money multiplier" slider is outside_mult.

Diminishing returns, with a fixed constant instead of a floor. A campaign that already has a lot of money gets less marginal benefit from one more dollar than a campaign still building out its operation. passes = money_eff / P_dollars is how many of these full-race impression passes the campaign's effective money buys. Rather than a flat dollar floor on money, every campaign is treated as already having reached its electorate many times over (prior_reach = 30) before any donation arrives — standing in for the reach a campaign gets for free, from its ballot party label, earned media, existing name recognition, and party infrastructure, and also keeping the first dollar's value finite. It is a judgment call with no rigorous empirical grounding, which is why it is fixed rather than a slider here. The marginal value of one more dollar is then (prior_reach + passes)−eta / P_dollars — the derivative, with respect to dollars, of a CRRA utility curve over total passes. It converges to the old money_eff−eta·Peta−1 shape once passes is large relative to prior_reach, but stays finite as money approaches $0 instead of relying on an arbitrary floor. Default eta = 2/3 (0.67) sits inside the uncertainty of Jacobson (1990)'s Box-Cox estimate that challenger House spending is concave with lambda = 0.28 (SE 0.04), i.e. eta = 1 − lambda ≈ 0.72.

Putting it together.

  • raw = V × el × Nrel × (prior_reach + passes)−eta / P_dollars, where el is Silver's elasticity estimate for the race, entering here a second time as an ease-of-persuasion multiplier (it already widens the margin distribution used inside a, above).
  • impact = raw / mean(raw), where the mean is taken over every race on the board — scored or not, shown by the current filters or not — so the average race scores 1.00, not the top one, and a race's score doesn't move just because you filtered the table down to a smaller set. A race scoring 5.00 is expected to do about five times as much per dollar as the average race; impact is displayed to two decimal places.

Races the model cannot score at all (no defined tipping-point probability) show an em dash instead of a number and sort to the bottom of the table. They still count toward the denominator above (as does every race excluded from the board entirely — see the note below the table) with a raw score of zero, so they can't inflate other races' scores by shrinking the average. This is a simplified, illustrative model built on public forecast and campaign-finance data. It is not investment or campaign advice, and no part of it should be read as a prediction of any individual race's outcome.
