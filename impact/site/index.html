2026 Marginal Impact Ranker

Which competitive U.S. House and Senate races give a Democratic donor the most impact per dollar — right now.

Impact is a marginal-impact-per-dollar score: how much one more dollar to a given race is expected to move the chance of flipping that seat and the chance that seat flips control of its chamber, discounted for how expensive it is to reach voters there and for how much money the campaign already has. Scores are normalized so the single highest-impact race among those currently shown always reads 100.0; every other race is relative to it. Definition of how much money they already have
What this is

Which FEC-derived figure stands in for "how funded is this campaign already" when computing diminishing returns. Cash on hand + projected contributions extrapolates each campaign's recent monthly fundraising rate out to Election Day; total receipts is everything raised so far this cycle; cash on hand is only what's currently banked. Whichever figure is chosen is floored so that a campaign with little or no reported money isn't treated as having infinite marginal value.

Methodology

The model. For each race, let a be the modeled probability that one additional vote flips that seat, and b be the modeled probability that one additional vote in that seat flips control of its chamber. A race's total value is V = c·b + w·a, where w is 1 for a House seat or the Senate-seat-value slider for a Senate seat, and c is the matching chamber-control weight slider.

a — chance one vote flips the seat. Silver Bulletin's forecast gives a win probability for the leading Democrat (or, where there is none, the leading independent). That probability is converted to an implied margin distribution (assumed roughly normal, with a race-specific spread derived from Silver's own elasticity estimate), and a is the density of that margin distribution at a perfectly tied race, times one over the size of the race's electorate. Intuitively: a race Silver already calls near a toss-up, with relatively few voters, has a much higher chance that a single vote is decisive than a lopsided race or one with a huge electorate.

b — chance one vote flips the chamber. This is Silver Bulletin's own Voter Power Index for the race — the modeled probability that this specific seat ends up being the tipping-point seat for chamber control, per vote cast there. It is largest in races that are both close and pivotal to the chamber majority.

Reach cost. A dollar spent on broadcast advertising pays for impressions across an entire media market, not just the district, so in a district that shares an expensive market with other, more populous or more numerous constituencies, much of that spending reaches voters who cannot cast a vote in this race. Reach cost is that per-constituent media-market overspill factor multiplied by the relative size of the race's own electorate (median House district = 1.0) — in other words, what it actually costs a campaign to put a message in front of a fixed share, here 1%, of its electorate. The theta slider blends that reach cost with a "fully targetable, equal everywhere" cost of 1.0: reachEff = theta + (1−theta)·reach.

Diminishing returns (CRRA). A campaign that already has a lot of money gets less marginal benefit from one more dollar than a campaign that is still building out its operation. We model this with a constant-relative-risk-aversion (CRRA)-style power discount on the campaign's existing money, money−eta, and the same discount applies to reach cost with the complementary exponent, reachEffeta−1 — so a race that is expensive to advertise into needs more dollars to have the same effect as a cheap one. "Money" itself is whichever definition is selected above, floored at $1,000,000 (see money_floor in the data) so that a campaign with almost no reported money isn't treated as having spuriously infinite marginal value.

Putting it together.

  • raw = V × money−eta × reachEffeta−1
  • impact = 100 × raw / max(raw), where the max is taken over whatever races are currently shown by the chamber and search filters — so the top race in view is always 100.0.

This is a simplified, illustrative model built on public forecast and campaign-finance data. It is not investment or campaign advice, and no part of it should be read as a prediction of any individual race's outcome.
