2026 Marginal Impact Ranker

Which competitive U.S. House and Senate races give a Democratic donor the most impact per dollar — right now.

Impact is a marginal-impact-per-dollar score: how much one more dollar to a given race is expected to move the chance of flipping that seat and the chance that seat flips control of its chamber, discounted for how expensive it is to reach voters there and for how much money the campaign already has. Scores are normalized so the average race on the whole board scores 100 — a race scoring 300 is worth about three average races, one scoring 16 about a sixth of one — and that average is fixed across the full race set, so scores don't shift just because you filter or search. Model assumptions

Each control below has its plain-English definition next to it. Change any of them and the table above recalculates immediately.

0.010.99
What this is

At 0, only the cost of reaching voters matters and the size of a campaign's existing war chest is ignored. At 1, only diminishing returns to the war chest matter and differences in the cost of reaching voters are ignored. In between, both count, with more weight on diminishing returns the higher this goes.

Default 0.6667 (2/3) draws on Jacobson (1990), whose Box-Cox estimate of the concavity of challenger House spending was lambda = 0.28 (SE 0.04); since votes scale as spendinglambda, eta = 1 − lambda ≈ 0.72. 2/3 is used here as a round default sitting within that estimate's uncertainty band.

0100
What this is

How much extra, measured in House seats, the tipping-point seat that decides control of the House is worth. At 25, winning the seat that flips the chamber is worth 25 ordinary House seats on top of the seat itself.

03
What this is

How much the tipping-point Senate seat is worth relative to the House control weight. 1.0 means the same control bonus applies to both chambers.

Default 0.80 because a non-tipping Senate seat can still move the tipping point in the next two cycles, and because the Senate has thresholds beyond a bare majority (60 for cloture, two-thirds for treaties and impeachment convictions), whereas the House is purely majoritarian with every seat contested every cycle.

130
What this is

A Senate term is 3× as long and each seat represents a much larger, harder-to-flip constituency (roughly 4.35× a House seat's share of a chamber), so a Senate seat is treated as worth several House seats in this model.

01
What this is

Share of a campaign dollar that can be aimed precisely (digital, connected TV, mail) rather than broadcast, which spills into an entire media market beyond just this race's voters. At theta = 1 every race effectively costs the same to reach a voter in; at theta = 0 the full media-market overspill penalty applies.

Based on Wesleyan Media Project data on 2026 congressional advertising, digital + connected TV run about 46% of ad spend in House races and 52% in Senate races, while AdImpact's 2024 full-cycle actuals put digital + CTV closer to ~35%. 0.40 splits the difference between those estimates. Reasonable range: roughly 0.15–0.55.

0200
What this is

Every campaign is treated as already having reached its electorate this many times before any donation, so no candidate is scored as having infinitely valuable first dollars. This stands in for everything besides the candidate's own reported cash that already puts messages in front of voters — party committees, outside spending and earned media — and it scales with how expensive the race is rather than being a flat dollar floor. At 60 that is roughly $2.6M of equivalent reach for a typical House race.

Definition of how much money they already have
What this is

Which FEC-derived figure stands in for "how funded is this campaign already" when computing diminishing returns. Cash on hand + projected contributions extrapolates each campaign's recent monthly fundraising rate out to Election Day; total receipts is everything raised so far this cycle; cash on hand is only what's currently banked. Whichever figure is chosen is used as reported, with no minimum floor — it's the "Head start" slider above, not this one, that keeps a campaign with little or no reported money from being scored as having infinitely valuable first dollars.

Methodology

The model. For each race, let a be the modeled probability that one additional vote flips that seat, and b be the modeled probability that one additional vote in that seat flips control of its chamber. A race's total value is V = c_eff·b + w·a, where w is 1 for a House seat or the Senate-seat-value slider for a Senate seat, and c_eff is the House control weight for a House seat, or the House control weight times the Senate control multiplier for a Senate seat (c_eff = c_house·senate_mult).

a — chance one vote flips the seat. Silver Bulletin's forecast gives a win probability for the leading Democrat (or, where there is none, the leading independent). That probability is converted to an implied margin distribution (assumed roughly normal, with a race-specific spread derived from Silver's own elasticity estimate), and a is the density of that margin distribution at a perfectly tied race, times one over the size of the race's electorate. Intuitively: a race Silver already calls near a toss-up, with relatively few voters, has a much higher chance that a single vote is decisive than a lopsided race or one with a huge electorate.

b — chance one vote flips the chamber. This is Silver Bulletin's own Voter Power Index for the race — the modeled probability that this specific seat ends up being the tipping-point seat for chamber control, per vote cast there. It is largest in races that are both close and pivotal to the chamber majority.

Reach cost, and turning it into dollars. A dollar spent on broadcast advertising pays for impressions across an entire media market, not just the district, so in a district that shares an expensive market with other, more populous or more numerous constituencies, much of that spending reaches voters who cannot cast a vote in this race. Reach cost (reach) is that per-constituent media-market overspill factor multiplied by the relative size of the race's own electorate (median House district = 1.0) — in other words, what it actually costs a campaign to put a message in front of a fixed share, here 1%, of its electorate. The theta slider blends that race-specific reach cost with anchor, the mean reach cost across scorable House races — a stand-in for "fully targetable, priced like an average race" spending: P_dollars = (theta·anchor + (1−theta)·reach) × K. K ($5,000) converts that index into an actual dollar figure — the price of one full "impression pass," touching every voter in the race once — calibrated at roughly $0.02 per impression times a ~250,000-voter median House district. So P_dollars is a real cost in dollars, not just an index: at the defaults, it runs from under $20,000 in the cheapest races to well over $100,000 in the most expensive ones.

Electorate size (Nrel). A dollar doesn't buy a fixed head count of persuaded voters, it buys a fixed fraction of a pass — the same theta/reach-priced share of the race's electorate regardless of that electorate's size. But that fraction of a bigger electorate reaches more actual people, so raw is scaled up by Nrel, the race's electorate size relative to the median House district. Net effect: electorate size cancels out of the seat term rather than mechanically favoring small districts, which is what would happen if a dollar instead bought a fixed number of persuaded voters.

Diminishing returns, with a head start instead of a floor. A campaign that already has a lot of money gets less marginal benefit from one more dollar than a campaign still building out its operation. passes = money / P_dollars is how many of these full-race impression passes the chosen money figure buys. Rather than a flat dollar floor on money, every race is given a "Head start" of prior_reach passes — equivalent exposure from party committees, outside spending and earned media that exists before any donation arrives, and which (because it's denominated in passes, not dollars) automatically scales with how expensive the race is to reach. The marginal value of one more dollar is then (prior_reach + passes)−eta / P_dollars — the derivative, with respect to dollars, of a CRRA utility curve over total passes. It converges to the old money−eta·Peta−1 shape once passes is large relative to prior_reach, but stays finite as money approaches $0 instead of relying on an arbitrary floor. Default eta = 0.6667 sits within the uncertainty of Jacobson (1990)'s Box-Cox estimate that challenger House spending is concave with lambda = 0.28 (SE 0.04), i.e. eta = 1 − lambda ≈ 0.72.

Putting it together.

  • raw = V × el × Nrel × (prior_reach + passes)−eta / P_dollars, where el is Silver's elasticity estimate for the race, entering here a second time as an ease-of-persuasion multiplier (it already widens the margin distribution used inside a, above).
  • impact = 100 × raw / mean(raw), where the mean is taken over every race on the board — scored or not, shown by the current filters or not — so the average race scores 100, not the top one, and a race's score doesn't move just because you filtered the table down to a smaller set.

Races the model cannot score at all (no defined tipping-point probability) show an em dash instead of a number and sort to the bottom of the table. They still count toward the denominator above (as does every race excluded from the board entirely — see the note below the table) with a raw score of zero, so they can't inflate other races' scores by shrinking the average. This is a simplified, illustrative model built on public forecast and campaign-finance data. It is not investment or campaign advice, and no part of it should be read as a prediction of any individual race's outcome.
