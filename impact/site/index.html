2026 Marginal Impact Ranker

Which competitive U.S. House and Senate races give a Democratic donor the most impact per dollar — right now.

Impact is a marginal-impact-per-dollar score: how much one more dollar to a given race is expected to move the chance of flipping that seat and the chance that seat flips control of its chamber, discounted for how expensive it is to reach voters there and for how much money the campaign already has. Scores are normalized so the single highest-impact race among those currently shown always reads 100.0; every other race is relative to it. Methodology

The model. For each race, let a be the modeled probability that one additional vote flips that seat, and b be the modeled probability that one additional vote in that seat flips control of its chamber (its "vote power index"). A race's total value is V = c·b + w·a, where w is 1 for a House seat or the Senate-seat-value slider for a Senate seat, and c is the matching chamber-control weight slider.

a — chance one vote flips the seat. Silver Bulletin's forecast gives a win probability for the leading Democrat (or, where there is none, the leading independent). That probability is converted to an implied margin distribution (assumed roughly normal, with a race-specific spread derived from Silver's own elasticity estimate), and a is the density of that margin distribution at a tied race, scaled by how many effective voters stand between a tie and a flip. Intuitively: races Silver already calls near a toss-up have a much higher chance that a single vote is decisive than a race that is a lock either way.

b — chance one vote flips the chamber. This is Silver's own "vote power index" for the race — roughly, the probability that this specific seat ends up being the tipping-point seat for chamber control, per vote cast there. It is largest in races that are both close and pivotal to the chamber majority.

Diminishing returns (CRRA). A campaign that already has a lot of money gets less marginal benefit from one more dollar than a campaign that is still building out its operation. We model this with a constant-relative-risk-aversion (CRRA)-style power discount, money−eta: at eta = 0 money doesn't matter at all to the ranking; as eta rises toward 1, well-funded campaigns are discounted more and more heavily relative to under-funded ones. "Money" itself is whichever definition is selected (projected cash available through Election Day, total receipts, or cash on hand), floored at $250,000 so that a campaign with little or no reported money isn't treated as having literally infinite marginal value.

Media-market overspill cost index. A dollar spent on broadcast advertising pays for impressions across an entire media market, not just the district — in a district that shares an expensive market with other, more populous or more numerous districts, much of that spending is wasted on voters who cannot cast a vote in this race. The cost index captures that relative overspill per constituent. The theta slider interpolates between "all spending is broadcast and fully subject to this overspill" (theta = 0) and "all spending is precisely targetable digital/mail, so cost is equalized across races" (theta = 1). The blended cost, costEff, then enters the model the same way money does — as a diminishing-returns power term, costEffeta−1 — so a race that is expensive to advertise into needs more dollars to have the same effect as a cheap one.

Putting it together.

  • raw = V × money−eta × costEffeta−1
  • impact = 100 × raw / max(raw), where the max is taken over whatever races are currently shown by the chamber and search filters — so the top race in view is always 100.0.

This is a simplified, illustrative model built on public forecast and campaign-finance data. It is not investment or campaign advice, and no part of it should be read as a prediction of any individual race's outcome.
